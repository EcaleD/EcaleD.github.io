It is easy to see the appeal of an AI tutor. A child gets stuck, asks a question, and gets an explanation. Still confused? Ask again. No need to worry about holding up the class.

I like that possibility. I am less sure what to make of the moment just afterward, when the explanation feels clear and the child moves on. Would they know where to start with a slightly different problem tomorrow?

That is the part of AI and education I keep coming back to. In much of our work, getting something finished faster is a reasonable goal. With schoolwork, it is harder to separate the finished task from the practice it was meant to provide.

I do not have a settled view on where the balance should be. But a few studies have made the question more interesting to me, and less easy to answer with “AI is good for learning” or its opposite.

## When the homework looks better

A [high-school math experiment in Turkey](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) gave me pause. Nearly 1,000 students took part. With a basic GPT-4 interface, students scored 48% higher on practice work than those without it. That sounds encouraging—until the later tests, when AI was no longer available. Then the same group scored 17% lower than the control group.

The experiment also tried a version guided by teacher-provided material and instructions to offer hints. That largely avoided the drop, though it did not produce a statistically significant improvement on the unassisted tests either.

It was a short experiment at one school. I would hesitate to turn it into a general claim about AI tutors. Still, it makes me wonder how often we might mistake better-looking work for better understanding.

A wrong answer at least leaves a clue. A teacher can see where a child added the wrong numbers or misunderstood a rule. An answer produced with help might make that confusion harder to spot. Perhaps one of the less obvious challenges will be keeping those gaps visible, even as the work becomes easier to finish.

## How much help is enough?

My first reaction to that result was to think that AI should give fewer answers. But “let the child struggle” does not seem like much of a solution on its own.

There is [research on students learning electrical-circuit troubleshooting](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cedpsych.2010.10.004) in which studying worked examples led to better learning with less mental effort than solving problems without that support. Sometimes seeing how to do something is exactly the help a beginner needs.

Take a child who adds one-third and one-quarter and gets two-sevenths. Showing why the pieces need to be the same size could help. Repeatedly asking “What do you think?” might just be frustrating.

What I would be curious to see is what happens on the next problem. Does the explanation give the child a way in? Or do they need the whole thing explained again?

This makes personalization sound less straightforward than it first appears. An AI tutor might be very good at explaining fractions through football or dinosaurs. Knowing when to explain, when to offer a hint, and when to wait seems like a different problem. I am not sure how reliably today's systems can make those judgments.

## Who notices the confusion?

There is a related question that bothers me a little. Suppose the tutor chooses every exercise, spots every mistake, and decides when it is time to move on. Where does the child get to practice noticing their own confusion?

That is a concern I have, not a finding about what AI will do to children. A system might help children practice this instead: ask them to choose a next step, explain why a problem was difficult, or say what they are still unsure about.

The familiar advice to “check the AI” feels incomplete here too. In the fraction example, knowing that two-sevenths is smaller than one-third gives the child a reason to question the answer. Without that understanding, what would they check?

I find it hard to imagine learning to use AI well without also learning enough of the subject to disagree with it. How much is enough, and how children build that knowledge while using these tools, still feels open.

## More than an explanation

With younger children, I become even less sure that a correct answer tells us very much about the whole experience.

In a [study of 90 children aged three to six](https://escholarship.org/content/qt7544r8x5/qt7544r8x5.pdf), children who had guided story conversations with an automated agent scored similarly on story comprehension to those who talked with an adult. But children gave longer, more varied, and more relevant verbal responses with the adults. This was a structured, scripted agent, not a modern generative chatbot.

What interests me is the difference between those two observations. The children could understand the story similarly while having different kinds of conversations about it. A comprehension score only captures part of what happened.

I would not know how to extend that result to years of growing up with an always-available AI companion. That seems a much bigger question than whether a tool helps during one reading session.

It also brings up things that do not fit neatly into a learning score. What does the system remember about a child? How does it explain its limits? Does it leave room for the child to turn to other people? [UNICEF's guidance on AI and children](https://www.unicef.org/innocenti/reports/policy-guidance-ai-children) helped broaden my thinking here, beyond tutoring performance to privacy, safety, and children's ability to make choices.

## Where the adult fits

One approach I find encouraging is to give AI to the person helping the child.

In the [Tutor CoPilot trial](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai24_1054_v2.pdf), human tutors were given access to AI suggestions during math sessions. The working paper reports that their students were four percentage points more likely to pass end-of-session assessments than students whose tutors were not given access. That does not tell us whether the benefit lasts across a school year, but it is an interesting starting point.

Here, the tutor was still there, deciding what to say next. I can imagine this being useful without having to imagine a child doing all their learning alone with a chatbot.

I also wonder how differently the same technology might be used in different schools. In one, it could give teachers more time for individual attention. In another, it might become a reason to provide less human support. Both could be described as expanding access to AI, while feeling very different to the children involved.

For now, I am drawn to the possibility of easier access to patient, useful help. I would not want caution about AI to turn into nostalgia for being stuck with nobody available to explain something.

Better homework would be encouraging. I would still want to see what the child tries next, what they can explain, and where they need someone beside them.

Perhaps that is the question I would start with: after a good explanation, what has become possible for the child that was not possible before?
